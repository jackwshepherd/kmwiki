# AI Productivity and Hidden Costs

**Summary**: AI brings genuine value, but also its own baggage — an obsession spiral, needless improvement loops, and the risk of replacing activities (like writing) that are themselves valuable thinking tools.

**Created**: 2026-05-27

## The Honest Question

AI does amazing things. But how much more effective has it actually made us day-to-day? The answer is hard to quantify — partly because we're not meticulous about benchmarking before and after, and partly because AI brings its own baggage that offsets some of the gains.

Three sets of baggage to keep in mind:

---

## #1 The AI Obsession Spiral

The pattern: you start using AI for a specific purpose → the output isn't quite right → you try to improve it → you build tooling around the improvement → the tooling needs more features → you lose track of why you started.

**Example:** Needed diagrams and UI mockups to support an article on KM and agentic AI. Previously would have made these in Figma. Decided to try Claude Code instead. The output wasn't as good as Figma (things get lost in translation between prompt and output; with images, it's very hard to refine specific things without recreating the entire image). So: built a UI to organise the diagrams → added a Copy Image function → added colour gradients → animated them → built a Copy Animated GIF function → spent time debugging the GIF function.

Total time: over an hour. Original purpose: forgotten. Will the diagrams actually appear in the article? Maybe.

> *Beware the AI obsession spiral.*

This is the same dynamic as the vanity project in [[prototypes-and-disposable-apps]]. The AI tooling becomes the project, not the thing you were trying to get done.

---

## #2 Needless Improvements

The AI review loop: use AI to do a paragraph-by-paragraph review of something you've already written, incorporate the feedback, feel great as if a respected colleague has thoroughly vetted the work.

Reflection: AI picked up a few embarrassing typos and clumsy phrases. The rest was largely tinkering. The value wasn't really in improving the work — it was filling a *reassurance hole* left by the absence of a human reviewer you actually respect.

The prevailing discourse focuses on whether AI gets you 80% of the way there. The more interesting question: what if it's *you* who got it 95% of the way there, and the remaining 5% added by AI wasn't worth 30 minutes of back-and-forth?

> *Be targeted. Apply scrutiny about whether AI is providing value or just delivering meaningless reassurance.*

---

## #3 The Value of Thinking

An experiment: for one article on contract drafting, fed Claude all prior writings, tweets, and LinkedIn posts, and asked it to draft an article based on a detailed essay plan. The output was fairly good and raised a few points not originally considered.

Published it. Then felt uncomfortable.

There's already a race to the bottom on social media — a focus on producing a lot of half-baked content instead of smaller amounts of high-quality, thoroughly thought-through content.

But the deeper issue: *for me, the main benefit of writing is that it's a medium to help think.* Getting AI to pad out a half-baked essay plan means losing the medium that makes the thinking happen. When you write, you formalise your thinking and see it differently on a page. That's valuable. If you have AI replace the writing, you lose the thinking process that the writing was serving.

> *If you write to think, use AI to prompt new ideas and check your work — not to replace your writing process.*

---

## The Synthesis

AI is great. But we need to be:

1. **Targeted** — not going down the obsession spiral, not building AI tooling for its own sake
2. **Honest** — applying scrutiny about whether we're getting real value or just reassurance
3. **Protective** — never replacing activities that help us think and learn, even if AI could technically do them

The question isn't whether AI is impressive. It's whether using AI in a given situation is actually the best use of your time and cognitive capacity.
