# Legal Tech Adoption

**Summary**: Technology is useless if nobody uses it. Getting legal tech adopted requires understanding why people should care (pull factors), what compels them to act (push factors), building trust, and communicating in terms of user value — not tool features.

## The Graveyard of Unused Legal Tech (Shelfware)

Technology needs users. The graveyard of unused legal tech — where legal tech goes to die — is a real problem:

- **Financial**: money spent on tools nobody uses, often undetected because usage metrics aren't tracked
- **Resource**: security reviews, development integrations, support onboarding — all wasted
- **Trust**: lawyers see emails about new tools but no usage among peers. They start ignoring all new tech communications. That trust damage compounds over time.

The instinct is to blame clunky interfaces and bad design. But lack of adoption is rarely a software problem — it's a missing piece problem.

> *Users + missing piece + technology = value*

The missing piece is understanding why people should care — and creating conditions where they do.

## Excuses vs Reasons

When pushing new technology in law firms, you'll hear:

- *"I'm a bit of a luddite"* / *"Talk to the younger people"* → masking discomfort with change itself
- *"I'm too busy"* → often valid, but sometimes an excuse
- *"This only works if everybody uses it"* / *"This requires a culture change"* → sometimes genuine, often displacement
- *"It needs to work on my phone"* / *"It needs to integrate with X"* / *"I won't use it if I have to remember another login"* → feature requests that multiply endlessly

The test: if users raise concerns without having invested any time in the technology, addressing each concern is whac-a-mole. Solve one and another appears. The underlying issue is usually that they haven't been given a reason to care — and so every raised concern is a way of saying "no thanks" politely.

**Dig deeper.** The underlying reason is nearly always discomfort with change itself. The path forward is not to argue with the surface-level objections.

## The Zone of Apathy

The zone of apathy is the gap between "this exists" and "I will change how I work to use it."

The common sentiment: *"We will never adopt something new unless we can see that the value outweighs the burden of changing."*

People sizing up whether to cross this zone are weighing: the burden of learning a new tool, the difficulty of bedding it in, the uncertainty of whether it will be worth it. The only way to convince them is to build trust and give them a reason to care.

Telling them they're stuck in the dark ages doesn't work. Preaching about the future of legal tech doesn't work. Being liked and being trusted is what opens the door.

## Pull Factors (Bottom-Up)

Pull factors are individual-level reasons to change. They are the things you can point to that make people *want* to use the tool because it makes their specific life better.

**How to discover them:** Speak to the people you've identified as affected by the process area. Ask them to walk you through how they currently work. Map the nuances. Identify the genuine pain points — not the dinner-party description of their job, but the unspoken mundane.

**Why this gets pushback:**
- *"You're the expert, why do you need to ask?"* — Your experience is not representative; you're already sold on the tool; the task is to make others care
- *"People don't have time"* — If they won't spare 15 minutes to talk about a problem, this is a red flag for adoption
- *"Show them a demo first"* — The goal is not to get feedback on a product; it's to establish whether the problem is significant enough to matter

Unless you speak to people directly, you're guessing at their motives — or worse, imputing your own motives onto them. **This is one of the principal causes of legal tech adoption failure.**

**Communication that works:**

| Approach | Example |
|----------|---------|
| Tool-centric (doesn't work) | "Microsoft Teams is now available. It lets you call, chat, and video conference." |
| User-centric (works) | "You've told us you spend hours each day filing emails and losing track of threads. We have a tool that solves this." |

In the user-centric version, the tool isn't even named. The value and problems take centre stage. This is nearly always a more effective communications strategy.

## Push Factors (Top-Down)

Sometimes pull factors are hard to establish — the change may benefit the organisation without individual-level benefit. For these cases you need push factors: business imperatives that compel action even without personal enthusiasm.

Examples of push factors:
- **Security or compliance requirements** — the existing tool isn't safe, the firm must move
- **End-of-life of existing system** — migrate or lose support
- **Burning platform** — a problem that will cause real harm if not addressed
- **Fee pressure** → the most powerful legal tech push factor

**Fee pressure as a push factor:**

In an uncapped billable hour model, time spent = profit. Efficiencies reduce time recorded = reduced profit. Technology doesn't obviously benefit the firm.

But when clients challenge fees or impose caps, time spent beyond the cap = money directly lost. Any efficiency here directly increases profitability. Practice areas under fee pressure are a goldmine for legal tech advocates — the ROI calculation becomes concrete and measurable.

> *Preventing fee cap excess is a great way to show the value of technology.*

Strong push factors without pull (or vice-versa) can sometimes be enough. But the ideal is both: individual reasons to care + organisational imperative.

## Business vs Personal Incentives

When investigating pull factors, distinguish between two types:

**Business incentives** — the firm is losing money unless things change. Examples: clients challenging time spent on a specific process; fixed-fee pressure; end-of-life of an existing tool. Business incentives often surface the most powerful push/pull combination: a message from a partner ("we must use this tool") combined with an underlying client-level reason to care. Deference and hierarchy are lawyer personality features — use them.

**Personal incentives** — individual pain that a tool alleviates. These aren't always apparent to the person experiencing them. A lawyer who says signings are fine may, when probed, reveal they go home late, can't sleep worrying about errors, and feel constantly stressed during closings. That's personal pain that a tool can address.

The work to find personal incentives is process mapping: understand exactly how people do a given task, step by step, and identify where the pain actually lives. Lawyers often describe their work at a dinner-party level and don't volunteer the granular pain. That's the researcher's job to surface.

## Selling the Tool: Lead with the Incentive, Not the Name

The standard launch email ("Microsoft Teams is now available — click here to access") fails for three reasons:

1. It talks about the tool, not the incentive. The tool is just the means.
2. It's vague. Would a caveman understand instantly what benefit this provides and what to do? (the "grunt test" — Don Miller)
3. It's a single point in time. If the email is deleted, the communication fails.

The alternative:
- Lead with the problem lawyers have told you they have: "You told us you spend hours compiling signature pages and go home late during closings..."
- Name the incentive, not the tool
- Start communicating before the tool is bought — bring lawyers along for the ride, include them in demos and purchasing decisions

Communication should happen before, during, and after deployment — not as a one-time announcement.

---

## Launch Strategy: Incremental Over Big Bang

Two consistent mistakes in tech launches:

**The big bang release** — launching to everyone at once. Guarantees that teething issues hit the largest possible audience. Bugs, workflow questions, and edge cases all surface in front of the whole firm. Reputational damage from a bad first experience compounds over time.

**The after-the-fact launch plan** — planning communications and training only after the tech is deployed. This wastes the best signal: you can write your elevator pitch *before* buying the tool, put it in front of lawyers, and see if it resonates. If nobody cares about the problem, don't buy the tool. You don't need to spend the money to find this out.

The right approach: incremental rollout (small group → resolve issues → next group → build momentum); launch planned before purchasing. Testimonials from early adopters act as social proof that reduces zone-of-apathy friction for subsequent groups.

---

## Adoption Framework Summary

1. **Identify who is affected** — not just lawyers who use the tool, but all stakeholders (partners, clients, support teams)
2. **Speak to them** — understand the actual workflow, the pain points, the unspoken mundane
3. **Map pull factors** for each group — what makes their individual lives better?
4. **Identify push factors** — is there a business imperative? Fee pressure? Compliance?
5. **Build trust** — be likeable, be helpful, don't preach
6. **Communicate in user-centric terms** — start with the problem the user has, not the tool you're selling
7. **Measure usage** — track adoption metrics; if nobody is using it, find out why before spending more money
