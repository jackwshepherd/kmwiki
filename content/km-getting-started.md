# KM Getting Started

**Summary**: The firms that succeed with knowledge management resist the temptation to jump straight to solutions. Start by defining what KM means for you, identifying specific use cases, auditing your data, and building the cultural and process infrastructure — then deploy AI as an accelerant.

## The Most Common Failure Pattern

"We want to do knowledge management." A firm that knows it has a problem with how knowledge is captured and shared, but is struggling to work out where to start. Sometimes this conversation ends with a technology purchase. Other times it ends with ambitious plans that never get off the ground.

The firms that succeed resist the temptation to jump straight to solutions.

---

## Step 1: Define What You Mean by Knowledge Management

This sounds obvious, but it matters. KM means different things to different organisations, and how you define it shapes everything that follows.

**The European vs US divide:**

| Approach | Focus | Implication |
|----------|-------|-------------|
| European | Content project — curated collections of high-quality work product for reuse | PSL-led, human curation, taxonomy investment |
| US | Technology project — large volumes of existing data made more searchable and accessible | Tech-led, search over unstructured content |

Most firms sit between these poles. The most effective approaches combine elements of both. But you need clarity on where you sit, because it affects: the resources you need, the processes you put in place, and the technology you buy.

If you skip this step, you risk talking at cross-purposes with stakeholders, vendors, and your own team — and you can't measure success because you haven't defined what it looks like.

---

## Step 2: Identify Specific Use Cases

"Do knowledge management" is too broad to be actionable. Get granular about what people actually need.

**Examples of use cases (and the different approaches they imply):**

- Lawyers struggling to find precedent for niche transactions → curate and categorise best work product
- Lawyers re-answering questions already answered elsewhere → invest in knowledge content (guides, playbooks, know-how)
- Lawyers reinventing the wheel on similar deals → deal intelligence capture, matter debriefs

Getting specific also helps you prioritise. Pick the use cases where you can demonstrate value quickly. Build from there.

**How to discover use cases:** Talk to people. Understand the specific points in their workflows where they struggle. Design your approach around those pain points. Don't ask lawyers abstractly what tech they need — ask them to walk you through what they actually do.

---

## Step 3: Understand Your Data

Whatever use cases you settle on, think carefully about your data: what do you need, where is it, and what condition is it in?

For most firms, the answer is that their data sits in a DMS alongside millions of files — irrelevant drafts, duplicates, abandoned work product. The challenge is separating what is useful from what is noise.

**Panning for gold:** Your DMS is the riverbed. Useful documents are the gold nuggets. There are three approaches to extracting them:

| Approach | How |
|----------|-----|
| Direct extraction | Rules-based or ML algorithm cherry-picks documents based on fixed criteria |
| Indicators | Characteristics that suggest a golden nugget (naming conventions, e-signature certificates, classification) — creates a smaller, less-noisy sub-set |
| Human curation | Processes that allow humans to earmark documents as golden nuggets |

The temptation: "let's just point AI at everything and let it sort itself out." The problem: AI can help with categorisation, but curation — the qualitative judgment about whether a document is reliable and reusable — still requires human involvement. Quality in, quality out. See [[knowledge-management-in-law]] for the curation/categorisation/contextualisation triad in detail.

**Before buying a tool or building anything: audit what you have.** Understand where the good content lives, how easy it is to identify, and what processes you might need to put in place to capture it going forward.

## Step 4: Culture and Process Before Technology

This is where most KM initiatives go wrong. Teams get excited about a platform, buy it, deploy it, then wonder why nobody uses it. The technology is rarely the problem.

Getting lawyers to share work product requires more than a button in software. It requires:
- People thinking of sharing as part of their job
- Leadership reinforcing that expectation consistently
- Knowledge sharing woven into processes lawyers are already doing

**Examples of weaving capture into existing workflows:**
- Matter debriefs after significant transactions — a natural moment to capture knowledge
- Transaction management tools with "deal bible" features — the closing process itself becomes a trigger
- The most successful programmes attach to workflows that already exist, so sharing is a by-product of something useful rather than an additional burden

---

## Step 5: Where AI Fits

AI has a growing role — categorisation of documents, extraction of metadata, improved search, drafting knowledge content, surfacing relevant materials at point of need.

But AI is best understood as an **accelerant for good KM practices**, not a replacement for them. Firms with well-structured, well-labelled knowledge bases get far more value from AI than firms that throw their entire DMS at an LLM and hope for the best.

> *Get your foundations right first. Define what KM means, settle on use cases, understand your data, build cultural and process infrastructure. Then use AI effectively.*

For the specific question of agentic search and whether it changes these requirements, see [[agentic-search]] — short answer: it doesn't remove the need for curation.

---

## Step 6: Start Small

The biggest risk with any KM initiative is trying to do too much at once.

Pick one practice group, one document type, one use case. Prove the value. Learn what works and what doesn't. Expand.

KM is a long-term investment. Treat it as an ongoing programme, not a one-off project. The first step doesn't have to be the biggest — it just has to produce something you can learn from.

## The Business Case for KM

Key ROI arguments (from a Knowledge Base business case framework):
- **Reduce write-offs** — lawyers exceeding budgets because they're not tapping into others' knowledge; good starting points dramatically reduce costs
- **Improved firm intelligence** — benefiting from everyone's experience leads to better client insights
- **Reduce risk** — prevents mistakes, especially repeated ones
- **Consistency** — reduces variation in client experience across different lawyers/offices
- **Training and development** — culture of learning; improved retention
- **Future-proofing** — structured, labelled knowledge bases get far more value from AI tools; essential for grounding generative AI
- **Agentic workflows** — a well-structured knowledge base can be consumed by AI agents via MCP; firms investing in curation today are positioned for agentic workflows tomorrow
