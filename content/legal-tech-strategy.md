# Legal Tech Strategy

## The Unspoken Mundane

The work lawyers talk about at dinner parties — arbitrations, complex negotiations, landmark deals — is not what they spend most of their time doing. The bulk of a lawyer's day is the *unspoken mundane*: everything they don't want to talk about when people ask about their job.

Examples at the junior end: comparing documents, preparing court binders, quadruple-checking the correct version was attached to an email, merging comments from three people on different versions of the same document, checking em-dashes and semicolons are consistent. At the senior end: writing time narratives, reading emails to check nothing has slipped, giving stylistic input on long Word documents, apologising when junior lawyers compared the wrong versions.

Due diligence is a good example. The core task sounds interesting: lawyers read contracts and flag risks. But the unspoken mundane swamps it: time recording, document allocation to avoid duplication, consolidating findings from multiple people, reformatting from Word to PowerPoint. The actual reading of contracts is the sexy part, surrounded by process.

> *The unspoken mundane is a technical debt in the legal industry that has been left unaddressed for far too long.*

The implication: legal technology that tries to tackle legal tasks based on a dinner-party description — rather than addressing the unspoken mundane — will often fail. Technologies that focus on things people actually care about fixing have a much better chance.

## Vapourware, Shelfware, and Solutions in Search of a Problem

Deploying AI without a thought-out strategy for where it can specifically help produces one of three outcomes:

- **Vapourware** — technology that doesn't exist or doesn't work
- **Shelfware** — technology that exists, is sold, but is never used
- **Solutions in search of a problem** — technology directed at meaningless problems, or in the wrong places

The two underlying failure modes that cause these:

**1. Unrealistic expectation setting** — futurists, academics, and vendors are motivated by speaking gigs, brand, and software sales. They drive urgency and play up benefits without addressing how you actually get value in practice. Lawyers get excited, buy, and get disappointed. Result: distrust of future change initiatives.

**2. Technology-first thinking** — organisations motivated by fear of being left behind oversimplify complex institutional problems by throwing expensive technology at them. Those who ask difficult questions about business models, incentives, and practical realities of change are cast as naysayers. The technology gets deployed; nobody uses it.

The better question frame:
- Instead of "what problems can AI solve for us?" → **what problems should we solve?**
- Instead of "what are the key use cases for AI?" → **what are the most effective solutions for the problems we should be solving?**

## Dinosaurs and Dreamers

Two sides polarise important discussions about legal technology:
- **Dinosaurs** — things are the way they are for a reason; no need to change
- **Dreamers** — lawyers will be disrupted unless they change how they work

Both positions are wrong in their extreme forms. The middle ground — weighing the importance of new technologies while ensuring they're used in appropriate ways and situations, and confronting practical roadblocks — is the least visible position because it's the hardest to make into a headline.

The preferred approach: a nuanced view of how technology works, what its benefits might be, and how those benefits map to the work lawyers actually do. Not blanket advocacy, not blanket skepticism.

## The Core Principle: Process Before Tech

> *When buying tech, counterintuitively, it doesn't make sense to start with tech. Instead, law firms and legal teams should adopt a structured and analytical approach around identifying tangible business problems to solve.*

The mistake repeated constantly: treating tech as the goal rather than the means. AI is not a goal in and of itself. Nor should it be a category of tech tools. The question is always: what problem are we solving?

A good idea to standardise and define processes *before* looking at tech. This helps be more specific about which parts to improve — and standardising and defining often improves processes without any tech at all.

## User Research: The Non-Negotiable Step

Law firms are often scared of doing research with actual lawyers and users before buying, deploying, or rolling out tech. The cost of skipping this step: a rollout that fails, or a senior partner who asks "did you involve any lawyers in this?" and the answer is no.

### What Doesn't Work
- Sending out surveys or emails asking what tech to deploy
- Asking lawyers to describe their processes in writing
- Showing Figma mockups or demos before understanding what you're actually solving

At the beginning of a project, before any research, things are uncertain and you don't even know what questions to ask. Asynchronous research (surveys, emails) won't give you the back-and-forth needed to dig into nuances or read emotional cues.

### What Works

1. Identify the groups and people affected by your proposal
2. Speak to 3 people in each group
3. Ask them to walk you through how they currently do the relevant process
4. Map the nuances into a workflow — people will say different things. Work out why.
5. Come up with a best-practice workflow. Sketch the pain points onto it.
6. Then ask: does the tech solve those pain points? Or better — what would solve them, tech or otherwise?

> *"I'm a lawyer already, I know this stuff" — maybe, but are you sure your problems are shared by everyone, and that your working practices are identical?*

Early adopters are often the ones who will complain if things go wrong. Fast-track their feedback into decision-making before going firmwide.

## The ROI Problem

Law firms are often asked to put metrics and numbers on the advantages of new tech. Two problems:

1. **Numbers never add up** — the processes being benchmarked are often so ill-defined and disparate that the baseline is meaningless. Associates will say their work depends on the whims of partners. Nothing is standardised.

2. **Numbers often don't matter** — why should a law firm care about saving 12,500 hours a year? Unless (a) clients complain about fees in that area, (b) they're losing pitches because they're too expensive, or (c) they work on a fixed-price basis — otherwise the number is irrelevant.

A better question: *how can we make life less hellish for lawyers and clients?* That still requires defining what makes it hellish, but it's a more honest starting point.

Firms that define higher-level metrics like "time to agreement" and map all processes that contribute to it can actually pinpoint what's driving their headline metric. That's the right level of measurement.

## Change Management

Change management keeps appearing in AI discourse — which is a good sign. But too often when people see "change management" what they hear is "bla bla bla random pointless process adding time and needless complexity."

The real blockers:
- Decisions made by partners in isolation from other employees
- Long debates over issues that could be resolved with objective research
- Tech deployed without enough user involvement

### Training Reframe

Training isn't just about *can people use the feature* — that should be a given unless it's very complicated. The value of training is making things **relevant**: when and why to use this, and with real use cases. That's what changes behaviour.

## Vendor Relations

Common failure modes:
- Vendors slating their competition (never effective; makes them look adversarial)
- Vendors throwing tech over the fence without considering whether or how it will be used
- Vendors presenting requirements checklists the firm developed without involving the vendor's knowledge of what works

Firms should involve vendors in building out requirements. Vendors have seen what works and what doesn't across many projects. That knowledge shouldn't be locked out of the process.

Conversely, there's real value in a law firm having a relationship with a vendor that goes beyond the product — someone who will think with them about business process, not just hand over software.

## The Legal Tech Echo Chamber

> *Is the echo chamber people saying the same high-level stuff over and over that either has no practical application or is so high level it ceases to carry meaning?*

The consensus exists in the industry. The actual people who need to change often aren't part of the discussion — or are ignoring it. The gap is not in understanding the concepts; it's in translating them into specific tasks, actions, and activities.

Too little content on the *how*. Too much on the *what*.

## Buying Legal Technology: Three Approaches

Most law firms buy legal technology using one of three approaches, two of which consistently fail:

**1. Buying what sounds cool**
Driven by social media hype, conference talks, and fear of being left behind. Signs: demos booked before problems are understood; no usage measurement post-launch; emphasis on look-and-feel over underlying jobs-to-be-done. Result: shelfware.

> *Buying what sounds cool might deliver a good press release or an impressive one-liner in a pitch. If your focus is on delivering actual change, it's a lazy approach.*

**2. The feature checklist**
A requirements document built by gathering features the team thinks they want. Problems: it approaches buying through the lens of technology (not value); it locks you into one feature approach when alternatives might solve the same underlying problem better. A firm fixated on "personalised dashboards" might reject a tool that solves the same discovery problem differently — and better.

**3. Outcomes and jobs (the right approach)**
Start with outcomes, not technology. Build an **outcome stack**: a hierarchy from a high-level business goal ("stop people leaving") down to a specific process ("optimise how we do signings and closings"). Work out what jobs people are doing at each level. Understand the actual workflow from the actual people doing it. Then identify what would solve their problems.

The outcome stack disciplines the buying process: it requires research, surfaces unknown problems, and keeps the focus on value rather than means. Most firms don't adopt it fully — but even partial adoption (always asking "what outcome does this serve?") improves buying decisions.

## Levels of Abstraction in Use Cases

When evaluating AI tools, use cases are often defined at the wrong level. There are two failure modes:

**Too high**: "AI will draft contracts for us." This encapsulates many tasks of varying difficulty, some of which AI can handle well and some of which it can't. Vague use cases produce vague evaluations, which produce wrong buying decisions.

**Too low without the high**: Deconstructing tasks without linking them back to the process improvement they're meant to drive. You end up optimising a task in isolation.

The productive approach: deconstruct high-level processes into their component tasks, evaluate which tasks AI can improve (and how much), and then judge whether those task-level improvements change the process outcome.

A practical example from contract drafting: "drafting a contract" breaks into (1) finding a starting point, (2) basic amendments, (3) tailoring to the situation, (4) reviewing comparables, (5) preparing a comparison, (6) incorporating comments. AI may be genuinely useful at task 1 and task 5 — but the value of solving those specific tasks must be weighed against the process improvement they drive overall.

Starting from the problem ("what is most valuable to solve?") rather than the technology keeps the analysis grounded. Use cases should emerge from problem understanding, not from a demo.
