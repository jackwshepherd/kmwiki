# AI Copilots in Legal Tools

**Summary**: Every legal tech vendor now has an AI copilot. Having one everywhere doesn't mean it's useful everywhere. The opportunity is in being selective, deliberate, and focused on the tasks that actually matter.

## The Current State

AI copilots have become table stakes in legal technology. Your DMS has one. Your e-billing platform has one. Your CLM tool has one. Even your time recording software probably has one. Each promises to save lawyers time, reduce manual effort, and unlock higher-value work.

This is not the same as all of them being useful.

The value of AI depends entirely on: (1) the task it supports, (2) the process it fits within, and (3) the context in which it is used. The proliferation of copilots has made this point more urgent, not less.

---

## The Feature Nobody Asked For

A pattern repeats in legal tech: a vendor builds a product that solves a genuine problem → they add features because the market expects it → eventually they add a feature that looks impressive in a demo but doesn't solve a problem anybody actually has.

AI copilots risk falling into this category for a lot of products.

**Example: transaction management platforms.** If you're using such a platform to manage a checklist of closing conditions, do you need a chatbot to help with that? Probably not. The value of the tool lies in its structure, and a chatbot adds ambiguity to something that is supposed to be unambiguous.

**Example: adoption-challenged tools.** If a firm is struggling to get lawyers to use a tool at all, adding AI is unlikely to fix that. The barrier to adoption is rarely that the tool isn't clever enough. It's usually that the tool doesn't fit how people work, or that nobody has invested in getting people to use it properly.

---

## When Copilots Do Add Value

There are genuine cases where embedding AI makes a lot of sense:

- **Search** — if you're looking for a document in a system holding millions of files, more intelligent AI-powered search can be transformative
- **Long document reading** — AI can help surface relevant information or summarise long documents so users can orient themselves more quickly
- **Drafting panels** — AI that sits alongside a document and helps refine language as you write, without generating the whole thing (see [[ai-in-contract-drafting]])

The common thread: AI supports a specific task within the tool, enhances something the user was already doing, and does so proportionately to the task at hand.

---

## The Evaluation Challenge

When every vendor has an AI copilot, firms face a new procurement problem. How do you compare AI features across products? How do you know whether the copilot in your DMS is better than the one in your contract review tool? Which ones should you actually encourage your lawyers to use?

Most firms don't have a good answer yet. They're dealing with a flood of AI features across their tech stack, with limited resources to evaluate them and limited bandwidth to roll them out. The result: a lot of AI features go unused.

---

## The Right Approach

Go back to basics:

1. **Work out where the real pain points are** for your lawyers
2. **Identify the tasks within those pain points** that AI could genuinely support
3. **Look at whether the AI features in your existing tools address those tasks** — if yes, invest in rolling them out properly; if no, don't use them just because they're there

The worst outcome of the copilot era would be for firms to become passive consumers of whatever AI features their vendors ship. That produces a scattergun approach where AI is everywhere but valuable nowhere.

We've been here before. Firms that got the most value from document automation were the ones that invested in understanding their processes and building the right templates. Firms that benefited most from KM invested in curation and content quality. AI is no different.

> *Every tool having a copilot does not mean every tool needs one. The opportunity lies in being selective, deliberate, and focused on the tasks that matter.*
